The premise — why this exists

所 以 然

Most classical Chinese, in modern editions, is annotated for the exam, not for the reader. Footnotes label characters; they rarely explain what the line is doing. Even native readers, when they sit with a difficult passage, often find that the official commentary has answered the wrong question.

So sometimes a reader writes to us with a passage they have lived with — a couplet from a grandmother's funeral scroll, four lines of Tao Yuanming, a single sentence in the Daodejing they keep coming back to — and asks for the kind of reading the schoolbook never offered.

This page is for that letter. You bring the passage and the question. We bring slow attention and a careful pen.

A small example · 為 例

One reader sent us five lines from Tao Yuanming's "Returning Home". She did not want a translation — she wanted to know why the third line breaks the pattern the first two had set. The reading we wrote for her was 4,200 words. It quoted four other Tang readings of the same poem, drew a short genealogy of the word 歸, and ended with the line her father had loved.

What you get back — three lengths, one rule

所 得

One reading, written for one reader. Delivered as a careful PDF, properly footnoted, with the source text reproduced in full. The length depends on the question, not on a tier.

短

Short reading

短 讀 · 約 兩 千 字

For a single line, a couplet, or a small fragment that has a clear question attached to it. Often one source-text reproduction, one careful translation, two or three pages of plain commentary, and one footnoted gloss.

≈ 2,000 words · 1 source · light footnoting
中

Standard reading

中 讀 · 約 五 千 字

For a short poem, a chapter of a classical text, or a question that needs one sideways comparison — a Tang reading set against a Song one, a Confucian word read inside a Daoist sentence. Most readings sit here.

≈ 5,000 words · 1–3 sources · careful footnoting
長

Long reading

長 讀 · 萬 字 以 上

For a difficult passage, a contested commentary tradition, or a request that asks the editor to walk all the way around the question. Includes a short bibliography and, where useful, a translator's note on the translation itself.

≈ 10,000+ words · 3–6 sources · full footnoting

One day — a quiet note on the future

後 話
後

For the first long while, every custom reading on this page is written by the editors of Ink & East. As the journal grows, we may open a small bench of outside readers — careful writers, vetted by the editors — who can take on briefs that match their training. You would always know who wrote your reading; the editors would always read it before it left.

This is a future shape, not a current offer. We are mentioning it here only because some readers have asked, kindly, whether this might one day exist. It might. Quietly, and after the journal is sturdy enough.
