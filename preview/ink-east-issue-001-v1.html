Wu Wei is not laziness.

無 為 · 非 懶
Editor's Note · by The Editors · 2026.04.20 · Beijing

This issue began with a sentence I did not write. A reader, in a letter we received last winter, asked: "Why do I feel guilty when I rest?" I read the letter four times. I made tea. I read it again. Around it, slowly, an issue began to form.

The Chinese tradition has a long argument with this question. Not a sermon — an argument. Laozi said the empty space inside the bowl is what makes the bowl useful. Zhuangzi told the story of a cook who never sharpened his blade because he had stopped fighting the meat. Tao Yuanming, after thirteen years in office, walked back to a small house and wrote five poems about returning. None of them said: do nothing. They said something quieter — do not force what does not yet need to move.

That is a sentence we have, in our century, almost lost the right to.

"The empty space inside the bowl is what makes the bowl useful."
道德經 · 第十一章Laozi · Chapter 11

We work to rest. We rest poorly, then call it lazy. We measure stillness in the same units as motion, and find it short. Most of what is sold to us as "rest" is in fact a softer kind of effort — a productive sleep, a working bath, a curated stillness. The old books would not recognise it. They would call it, plainly, more pushing.

— a footnote, kept
The Chinese phrase 無為 is often mistranslated as "non-action". A more accurate gloss is "unforced" — action that does not push the river, only follows where it has already begun to go. The classical English idea closest to it is not laziness, but tact.

So we have, in eight pieces, tried to listen to that argument plainly, in English, without dressing it up as anything other than what it is — a careful, patient idea about effort. There is a story, here, about a man who could not stop rearranging his room. There is an essay on why doing nothing is sometimes the hardest practice in a life. There are three short passages from Laozi, kept in their original Chinese alongside an honest English. There is a letter from a reader who rests but feels guilty, and a long, slow reply.

You will not find advice here. You will not find a method. You will not find a thing to buy that will fix the way you cannot rest. You will, I hope, find ways of seeing — old ways, that have outlived several empires and may outlive ours, kept as gently as we know how. That is, I think, what an old book is for.

Read in any order. Skip what does not speak today. Come back when something does.

The man who could not stop rearranging
his room.

不 停 整 理 房 間 的 人
A short story · by Lu Xian · Original · 5 plates of revision

He had been rearranging his room for eleven years. Not all the time — only at the edges of evenings, in the gap between dinner and not-quite-sleep, in the quiet half-hour after a phone call he had not wanted to take. He moved the desk three centimetres west. The lamp, by half an arm's length. He kept finding configurations that almost worked, and then, the next week, finding that they did not.

His mother, when she visited, said nothing. She put down her bag, sat on the bed, and looked at the corner where the chair had been. "You moved it," she said, kindly. He nodded. He could not have explained, then, why moving it had felt necessary.

Why doing nothing is sometimes
the hardest practice.

為 什 麼 休 息 最 難
An essay · by The Editors · Beijing, late winter

Sit in a quiet room for thirty minutes. Do not reach for your phone. Do not think about a project. Do not plan tomorrow. Notice what happens. Within four minutes — for most of us — a small panic enters the body. Within ten, an excuse appears, dressed as productivity. Within twenty, we have negotiated with ourselves and won permission to leave. We did not rest. We were chased out of rest by something the old books would have called 不安 — un-quiet — and we did not even notice the chase.

This essay is an attempt to take that chase seriously. To name what is doing the chasing. And to ask, with the patience of an older language, whether doing nothing is in fact the most demanding work a person can do — and why we have, in our century, almost forgotten how.

Three passages from Laozi,
for people who feel stuck.

老 子 · 三 章 選 讀
Classical Reading · edited & rendered by The Editors · Chapter 11 · 22 · 48

The Daodejing is the most translated Chinese book in the world, and the most over-translated. It has been turned into a self-help manual, a leadership pamphlet, a yoga inscription, a tea-towel. We have, in this plate, tried to do the opposite — to leave it small. Three short passages, in their original Chinese, with an English that does not pretend to be wisdom; only an honest reading of what each line is actually doing.

The three we chose all speak to people who feel stuck. "What is hollow can be used." — Chapter 11. "To bend is to be whole." — Chapter 22. "Day by day, less." — Chapter 48. Three sentences from a small old book, kept gently here in case one of them is the one you need today.

"I rest, but I still feel
guilty. Why?"

為 何 休 息 會 愧 疚
Letter signed · by "M., aged 31, Shanghai" · received Nov. 2025

The letter that started this issue. M. wrote, late one night, asking why every weekend felt like a debt — why even sleeping in registered, in her chest, as failing at something she had not been told to do. "I rest, and the rest of me does not rest with me. Why?"

The reply, written slowly over three weeks, threads through Laozi, Zhuangzi, a small story about a tea master, and a passage from the I Ching. It does not solve M.'s guilt. It does, we hope, name it more clearly than guilt usually allows itself to be named — and offer the company of an older argument that her chest is not, in fact, the first chest to have made this complaint.

Control, exhaustion, and the
ancient art of not forcing life.

不 勉 強 之 術
Patron · Long Read · by The Editors · 3 weeks of writing · 11 sources

This is the longest piece in the issue, and the slowest. It begins with a question that has bothered me for years: why does our generation, more than any before it, seem to confuse control with care? We control our calendars and call it discipline. We control our bodies and call it health. We control our partners and call it love. We control our own attention, second by second, and call it focus. And we are, almost without exception, exhausted.

The Chinese tradition has a different word for this kind of exhaustion. It does not call it burnout. It calls it 勉強 — miǎnqiǎng — forcing what does not yet wish to move. The plate that follows is a slow, threaded essay on what that word means, where it comes from, and what — if anything — the old books offer in its place.

A field note from Wudang:
walking with monks who do not hurry.

武 當 紀 行
Field Note · by The Editors · Wudang Mountains, Hubei · Feb 2026

I walked, in February, three days on the mountain. The monks did not hurry. They did not, also, walk slowly in the way that English-speaking yoga retreats teach you to walk slowly — performatively, attentively, as if each foot were a small ceremony. They walked at the pace of the path. The path went up; they went up. The path turned; they turned. When I asked one of them, on the second day, what they thought about while walking, he looked at me with the small confusion of a man who has been asked a question in a language he no longer speaks.

Six photographs, taken on a borrowed camera. A short note on what each is, and what it is not. Three sentences I overheard and could not stop turning over for the next month. A field note, kept honestly, with no morals attached.

Archive — three rest poems
from Tao Yuanming.

陶 淵 明 · 歸 去 三 首
Archive Note · selected & translated by The Editors · c. 405 CE

Tao Yuanming, after thirteen years of small offices, walked back to a small house. He wrote, in the years that followed, a body of poems that the Chinese have, for sixteen centuries, kept on a shelf within reach. They are not heroic. They are not sad. They are, mostly, about beans, mist, a gate, the way a road looks when one has finally stopped using it for going somewhere.

We have selected three. They close this issue because they are what the issue, in the end, is about — not the absence of work, but a different relationship with what work is for. Kept here in their original Chinese, with an English that does not pretend to improve them.

第 一 期 · 編 後 記

This issue took five months to make and ten weeks to write. Some of it was rewritten three times; one plate, four. We threw away an essay we had spent six weeks on because, on rereading, it had begun to perform calm rather than be calm.

An issue is, for us, a small object — about the weight of a paperback. It is meant to be read in one or two evenings, not all in a sitting. The character of this first issue, 無, was chosen by a reader's letter, not by us. The next issue's character will be chosen by our Patrons, in a small vote that opens at the end of this one.
